The Agent First Method

Design the work. Define the responsibility split. Then decide what AI belongs inside the system.

Agent First uses two complementary frameworks: a seven-step design process for changing a system, and a seven-layer diagnostic for understanding the decisions inside it.

Framework one · the design process

Human → Work → Knowledge → System → AI

01

Observe

Understand the real job, environment, goals, people, constraints, and workarounds.

02

Deconstruct

Expose decisions, rules, retrieval, handoffs, context switches, and failure modes.

03

Reassign

Decide what belongs to the human, the deterministic system, and AI.

04

Design

Shape the information, interaction, sequence, language, controls, and exceptions.

05

Prototype

Make the responsibility model tangible enough to test with real people.

06

Test

Compare the new system with a baseline and look for unintended burden.

07

Scale

Integrate only after the system has earned the right to become more complex.

Framework two · the decision environment

What is the person being forced to figure out in real time?

The diagnostic follows one decision through seven layers. The point is not that every layer needs AI. The point is to see where the surrounding system is failing to carry information, logic, or coordination that it could carry better.

01

Context

Question: What must be known about this customer, account, user, client, product, location, case, or situation before the decision can be made?

Failure mode: The person starts searching before the system has resolved which context matters.

02

Rules

Question: Which policies, constraints, permissions, exceptions, or business rules govern the situation?

Failure mode: The operating logic exists but is fragmented across documents, memory, training, and tribal knowledge.

03

Retrieval

Question: How hard is it to locate the right information at the moment of action?

Failure mode: The answer exists, but retrieving it is slow, cognitively expensive, or unreliable.

04

Decision

Question: What judgment genuinely belongs to the person, and what should already be resolved by the environment?

Failure mode: Routine interpretation is mislabeled as empowerment.

05

Communication

Question: What must be explained, how constrained is the language, and where is adaptation appropriate?

Failure mode: The person finds the rule but still has to convert it into usable communication on the fly.

06

Action

Question: What happens next—create, route, schedule, transfer, escalate, document, approve, decline, or stop?

Failure mode: Information is available, but operational follow-through is ambiguous.

07

Human Control

Question: What must remain visible, explainable, reviewable, and overridable by the human?

Failure mode: The system keeps a person technically “in the loop” without giving them meaningful authority or understanding.

The responsibility split

Keep human judgment. Eliminate unnecessary human burden.

The human should own

What requires humanity.

The exact boundary depends on the work, but these are common reasons to keep a person responsible.

  • Judgment under genuine ambiguity
  • Accountability
  • Relationships and trust
  • Creativity
  • Value-laden tradeoffs
  • Novel exception handling
The system should own

What technology can carry better.

This is the burden most likely to masquerade as “just part of the job.”

  • Retrieval
  • Memory
  • Context assembly
  • Deterministic rules
  • Version control
  • Coordination and computation
The design rule

Do not automate the worker’s workaround. Fix the reason the workaround exists.

A good Agent First intervention can result in AI, deterministic software, better information architecture, simplified policy, fewer systems, a prototype, training changes, or a combination. The method is intentionally technology-neutral until the work has been understood.
